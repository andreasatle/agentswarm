# Self-correction: a held-out benchmark, typed sentences and a reader critic

Started 2026-11-13, after the AI-workflows posts. The user asked whether the stream of small fixes makes a better swarm ("are we overfitting, creating a fix for every problem that doesn't generalize?") and whether the agents shouldn't find and correct errors themselves rather than Claude patching each one. Agreed plan (the user, 2026-11-13): a reader critic, structural rules only where they remove a whole kind of error, and Claude's role narrowed to bugs in the machinery (COLLABORATION.md rule 8); each measured on a held-out benchmark.

## The problem

Every fix of the last days was found on the run that showed the flaw and checked by a unit test, never by whether documents got better overall. Some removed a cause and generalize (word-cap rewrites, timeouts escalated, failed checks recorded, parallel teams). Others patch one symptom of free-text output: "v." and "J." treated as abbreviations, citations moved around by regular expressions, bracket tags trimmed. The writer returns free text and plain code guesses where sentences end; each patch improves the guess, none removes it. And form and clarity have no critic at all: the review step checks claims only, so Claude was the critic.

## The benchmark (evaluation/benchmark.py, benchmark.yaml)

Three held-out cases on sources already on disk, never used to find fixes: the LLM-scaling analysis (16 web pages and papers), the sleep tutorial (3 papers), the test-time-compute tutorial (5 papers), each at 2,000 words. `agentswarm benchmark <label>` writes each with the current code, answers its pauses (yes to sources and publishing, a failed section accepted so it counts), and appends one record per case to `~/agentswarm-data/benchmark.jsonl`:

- cost, minutes on the board, spend per model (the strong model's share shows escalations);
- the last citation audit's share of fully supported statements;
- reader-facing flaws found by a held-out judge (`ReaderJudge`: Sonnet 5.5, not part of any run), each with a verbatim quote that plain code checks, in four kinds: broken text, citation form, unclear, repetition.

A round costs about $2.50-4. Variance between rounds is unknown until a round is repeated; differences smaller than it are not results.

## The steps, each measured against the benchmark

Revised 2026-11-13 after a review by Codex, which the user brought in: it counted five prose heuristics in the document checker (several sentences on a line, a lowercase start, a start with punctuation or a citation, unbalanced brackets) and six repairs that guess what the writer meant (citations split, mapped to parts and moved, prose split into sentence lines). Moving a citation from the start of a line onto the previous sentence decides which sentence it supports: a semantic guess, to be removed. It also found that the review runs only when the citation audit falls below 90%, so a document with well-supported citations is never reviewed for anything else.

1. **Round 0, baseline:** the code as of 2026-11-13.
2. **Structured output:** the writer returns its section as typed parts (paragraphs as lists of sentences, each with the sources it cites; lists; equations), and plain code lays them out as Markdown. Its consumers change with it: the claim checker reads sentences and their citations from the structure instead of from line breaks, and the document check keeps only its structural rules (Markdown files, headings, text under them, citations naming real sources, a citation per section, the word cap). The sentence splitter, the abbreviation lists and the citation repairs are deleted with the prose rules, in the same change: until then they are the only guard against broken text. Accepted if the judge's flaws don't rise and cost doesn't rise beyond the noise.
3. **A mandatory quality review:** before publishing is put to the user, the assembled document is reviewed against its brief whatever its citation score: a reader and coherence perspective over the whole document (problems across sections included), beside claims. Findings carry a location, evidence and severity: a quote from the document, or for something missing, the part of the brief or outline that asks for it. The existing reviewer, referee and writer loop fixes confirmed findings; rounds are bounded, the repaired version is reviewed again, and what remains unresolved is shown to the user at the approval. Accepted if the judge's flaws fall by more than the noise for at most about $0.20 a document.

The judge stays outside every run: used in production it would stop being a held-out measure.

## Results

**Round 0, baseline (2026-11-13, `r0-baseline`), $4.27 in all:**

| case | cost | minutes | strong model | audit | judge's flaws: broken text / citation form / unclear / repetition |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| scaling | $1.80 | 18 | $0.41 | 100% | 0 / 5 / 7 / 3 = 15 |
| sleep | $1.22 | 10 | $0.13 | 74% | 0 / 2 / 5 / 2 = 9 |
| reasoning | $1.25 | 10 | $0.21 | 63% | 1 / 3 / 7 / 0 = 11 (review cut short: the day's $10 limit) |

What it says before any change: broken text is nearly gone already (1 flaw in 35); the flaws are mostly content-level, unclear passages (19) and repetition (5), with citation form (10) a distant second, mostly the same source cited twice in a row. So structured output addresses about a third of the judged flaws, and a reader review the rest. The reasoning case's review was cut short by the machine-wide limit, so its audit and flaws are not a clean baseline; it is rerun in the next round. Variance is still unknown.


**The mandatory review's cost, first seen (2026-11-14, `iter-engineering`):** the reader perspective over 9 sections reported 41 findings (3 a section, the cap), the referee confirmed 6, and ruling them took 93 referee calls (about 2.3 votes a finding, each with the whole document and about 2,300 tokens of reasoning): $1.17, against the plan's $0.20; the run's budget ran out before any fix. Change: the referee rules a review's findings together, one call a vote for all of them, only the undecided asked again: about 2-3 calls a section instead of 7. To be measured in round 1; if still far above $0.20, next is one reader review of the whole document instead of one per section (each already reads the whole document).

**Batched rulings, first seen (2026-11-14, `data-governance`, a literature study):** batching did not cut the cost: 45 ruling calls for 51 reader findings (9 confirmed), $1.50, about $0.03 a finding against $0.034 before. The cause is not the number of calls: 23 of the 45 ran to 5,000-16,384 output tokens ($1.36 of the $1.50), where the rest needed about 700. A probe of 5 real findings from that run (2026-11-15, $0.60) showed what they are: DeepSeek V4 Pro's own reasoning (15,605 of 16,222 tokens in one reply; some reach the output limit and are lost). OpenRouter's `reasoning.effort: low` and `reasoning.max_tokens: 3000` did not bound it (16,384 and 14,336 reasoning tokens); `reasoning.enabled: false` did (750-1,255 tokens, none cut off). Change: `review.finding_referee` names the model for findings that aren't cited statements, `referee-fast` (the same model, `thinking: false`); cited statements keep the reasoning referee (only 3 of its 97 calls ran long there, and checking a statement against a source is where reasoning should help). Not yet measured: whether rulings without reasoning are as good; the probe's verdicts varied between calls with and without it alike (the votes exist for that). Round 1 measures cost and the judge's flaws.

**Round 1 (`r1-typed`, 2026-11-15, $6.52): no measurement; two faults found.** Scaling and reasoning never finished: each spent $2.14 in 5 minutes, mostly GPT-Sol rewriting the last sections ($1.68 in 22 calls for scaling), because the document stayed 22-75 words over its 2,000-word cap. Cause: the typed writer counted its sentences' own words, while the document check counts the Markdown, where a cited sentence's full stop stands apart after the citation is removed and a bullet's dash is a word: 2-4% more. The outline's shares leave room only for the writer's 15% tolerance, so sections written to it overran the cap, and the writers could not see why. Fixed: `SectionLayout.words` counts the rendered Markdown as the check does, and a test runs the real check on the writer's count. Sleep finished: audit 58% (r0 74%), 11 judged flaws (r0 9), $2.24 (r0 $1.22), of which $1.13 the reader findings' rulings with reasoning on: the run's review configuration is written by the pipeline (`ProjectStage.reviewing`), which did not name `finding_referee`; now it does, tested. One case is not a measurement; round 2 reruns all three.

**Round 2 (`r2b-typed`, 2026-11-16; `r2-typed` before it stopped at once, Docker down, $0.25): no quality gain, about 1.6 times the cost.** Scaling $2.23 (r0 $1.80), audit 74% (100%), 13 flaws (15); sleep $2.18 ($1.22), 79% (74%), 13 (9); reasoning $2.47 ($1.25), 68% (63%), 11 (11). Scaling and reasoning hit the $2.50 case budget before their review or audit finished. Read from the boards, three causes. (1) Haiku cuts too little: after three "cut about N words" rewrites, 7 of 9 scaling sections were still 12-50% over their shares, the guide came to 2,150 words against a 2,000 cap, and GPT-Sol trimmed it eleven times to 2,001-2,025 ($0.74). (2) Revisions collided: the review made one revision task per finding, and the typed writer rewrites a section whole, so a reviewer's second finding on a section always conflicted with its first (11 conflicts in sleep and reasoning, none in r0, whose writer edited lines); each was redone, often by GPT-Sol. (3) A revision cut a 300-word section to its first sentence and passed every check (sleep, section 2; a later revision restored it). Also, every other GPT-Sol attempt failed with "Event loop is closed": langchain-openai keeps one HTTP client per address for the whole process, and a kept connection belongs to the event loop that opened it, so each pipeline stage's first call failed (reproduced against a local server).

**Fixes (2026-11-17).** The length is a target, not a cap (the user: "I see it more as a guideline"): the brief says "about N words" and the check fails only past 20% over (`GuideImport.SLACK`); the outline's shares stay scaled for the writer's tolerance. A reviewer's confirmed findings on a section become one revision task (`ReviewCampaign.fix_findings`), as claims already did. The writer refuses a revision that leaves less than half of the section (or of its share, if less) (`Writer.KEEP`). OpenAI-style models get an HTTP client that keeps no connection between calls (`ChatModels.async_client`). The benchmark records why a run didn't publish (`RunMeasure.unfinished`) instead of showing it as flawless. Not done: aiming Haiku's cuts lower, since the slack absorbs its overshoot; a per-model correction for writers' length errors goes to the backlog if a second writer model also misses its shares. Round 3 measures these.

**Step 2 built (2026-11-14): structured output and numbered references.** The writer answers with a `SectionDraft`: blocks (paragraphs of sentences, lists, displayed formulas), each sentence with its `cites` (source and page) as typed fields; `SectionLayout` writes the Markdown (heading, one sentence per line, citations inside the sentence before its final punctuation). A citation naming no source is sent back once with the names to use. Review revisions now reach the same typed path: each revision task carries its section's assignment (until now they went to a tool-using agent that edited the Markdown freely, past the writer's clean-up). Deleted with it: the sentence splitter and its abbreviation lists (`Sentences`), the citation repairs (`Citations`: spoken citations, pages cited as parts, grouped brackets, leading citations moved onto the sentence before), the assembly's move of citations after a full stop, and the document check's prose rules (one sentence per line, broken lines, abbreviations); the check keeps headings, text, real sources, a citation per section and the word cap. Assembly numbers each source by its first citation as a Markdown footnote [^n] (one number per source in a sentence, the pages cited listed in its entry), and the page shows [n] linking to the entry, its entry as a tooltip on hover, and one ↩ that returns to the [n] the reader followed. The page script is unchecked in a browser so far (the preview couldn't run a local file); the next document run's page will show it. To be measured in benchmark round 1 with the rest: the judge's flaws (citation form above all) and cost.
